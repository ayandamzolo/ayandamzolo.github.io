Integrations that can't fail silently

I own the path applications take from a lending platform to South African banks. When the platform changed submission vendors I designed the cutover so in-flight applications finished on the vendor already holding them while new ones routed to the replacement, so nobody resubmitted anything, and onboarding the next lender became configuration, not code. A companion service decides whether an application qualifies for a bank's new origination system or falls back to the legacy one, and reports every reason it fell back, not just the first.

Mortgage platform · primary author of 828 of its 968 commits. Laravel, SOAP, MySQL, Docker. The newer service has lint and tests in CI on every push.

Reading documents without trusting them

Applicants upload payslips, bank statements and offers to purchase. In production, OCR runs on a self-hosted container on our own infrastructure, with no third-party API key, and no client document leaves the network, and a language model turns that text into application fields. Which model reads which document type was decided by measurement, not preference: the cheaper model dropped names on identity documents and moved bank-statement expense totals by more than ten percent between two identical runs, so those types route to a stronger one and the documents that agreed stayed put.

Mortgage platform · PaddleOCR in Docker, Claude on Amazon Bedrock, Laravel queues. Field-hygiene guards are pinned to a sweep of over two thousand real documents, including one that stops a name like Nomvula being mistaken for the word "None".

Conversations that don't waste anyone's time

Money logic, and what happens when it changes

Sole author of the discount system for a travel booking platform: percentage and cash codes with geographic, product, day-of-week, minimum-cart and first-booking-only rules, modelled as three separate things: the code, the rule that governs it, and the redemption that records its use. The hard part is refunds. When a trip is modified after a code has been applied, the discount has to be recalculated against what the booking became, not what it was. I also wrote the business-logic document the stakeholders signed off from.

Finding what went quiet

When replies stop arriving, nothing errors. The work simply sits. This is the part I am best at. I run these as read-only investigations first: queries anchored on the business reference rather than a remembered row id, read before anything writes, and repair values copied from the upstream system's own stored response rather than from anyone's memory. Then the post-mortem gets written, because the next person deserves the map.

Across all three platforms. Eight production post-mortems and the vendor-cutover runbooks live in the repository, not in my head.

How I work

Six habits that show up in everything above, and the reason each one earns its keep. An octopus does not smash the jar. It works the lid, feels the mechanism, and gets in.

I go to the layer under the tool

When I wanted to understand a managed deploy platform, I rented a small droplet and did it by hand instead, so that PHP-FPM is a process manager I can reason about rather than a service that is either up or down, and so I know what a worker limit actually trades away.

That is why outage work lands. From a dashboard, a full disk is one red number. A level down it's one table holding almost all of it, and then you have something to actually fix.

The spec comes before the code

Integrations get written against the specification first, not reverse-engineered from whatever the first response happened to contain. Where ten things vary the same way, that's one interface with ten implementations decided up front, not ten near-copies discovered later.

I'd rather walk a requirements document and estimate it with the people who wrote it than receive a ticket and guess at what they meant.

I test demand before I build

Before building a billing feature I put up a landing page and took it to real customers, to find out whether anyone wanted it while it was still cheap to be wrong.

And I shelved an internal tool once I found the market already had a good version. Not falling in love with your own idea is harder than it sounds, and cheaper than the alternative.

I prototype in something you can click

My default way of explaining a concept is a working prototype rather than a document, because people judge an interaction properly once they've felt it, and argue about a paragraph describing one.

I stay on the line while I fix it

Through an outage I keep stakeholders updated the whole way rather than going quiet and returning with a resolution. People can plan around a problem they can see.

The same instinct applies to access: when a colleague needed data, I set them up with scoped read-only credentials of their own instead of handing over mine.

Then I write it down

Post-mortems for the failures worth understanding, runbooks for the procedures someone else will have to run at an awkward hour, and a business-logic document a stakeholder can sign off from. Knowledge that only lives in my head is a single point of failure.
