Document autofill

Applicants upload payslips, bank statements and offers to purchase; a queued job reads them with AWS Textract and fills the blank fields so nobody retypes a payslip. A document whose ID number contradicts the applicant's never overwrites anything — it stays a suggestion. Tax figures on payslips aren't suggested at all, because payslips mix monthly and year-to-date numbers and a confident wrong figure is worse than an empty field.

Uploaded documents are treated as data, never as instructions. AWS Textract, Laravel queues, S3.

Platform security and access

Multi-factor authentication and OTP across every login surface of a multi-tenant platform, switchable per tenant. Web application firewall rules and rate limiting in production, penetration-test findings tracked to remediation, and upload and download paths closed so tenant documents stop being reachable by anyone who guesses a URL.

WAF, NGINX hardening, audit logging, session invalidation on both guards.

Bank integration service

A service that decides whether an application qualifies for a bank's new origination system or must fall back to the legacy one, then routes it and tracks amendments and replies. An application that fails a rule is never rejected — it falls back, and the service reports every reason it did, not just the first.

Built solo. Laravel, Vue 3, Inertia, Pest. Lint and tests run in CI on every push.

Bank submission engine

The integration that submits applications and documents to South African lenders. When the platform changed submission vendors, I designed the cutover so in-flight applications finished on the vendor already holding them while new ones routed to the replacement — nobody resubmitted anything, and onboarding the next lender became configuration, not code.

Primary author of 558 of 651 commits. Laravel, PHP, SOAP, MySQL, Docker.

Partner data pipelines

Secure SFTP onboarding for partners who exchange files rather than call APIs, with schema validation at the boundary and access scoped per partner.

AWS Transfer Family over S3, IAM roles, CloudWatch logging. Redshift and DMS troubleshooting.

Finding the ones that went quiet

When a bank's replies stop arriving, nothing errors — the application simply sits. I work these as read-only investigations first: queries anchored on the business reference rather than a remembered row id, run and read before anything writes, and the repair values copied from the bank's own stored response rather than from anyone's memory.

Eight production post-mortems and the vendor-cutover runbooks live in the repo, not in my head.

How I work

Five habits that show up in everything above, and the reason each one earns its keep.

I go to the layer under the tool

When I wanted to understand a managed deploy platform, I rented a small droplet and did it by hand instead — so that PHP-FPM is a process manager I can reason about rather than a service that is either up or down, and so I know what a worker limit actually trades away.

That is why outage work lands. From a dashboard, a full disk is one red number. A level down it's one table holding almost all of it, and then you have something to actually fix.

The spec comes before the code

Integrations get written against the specification first, not reverse-engineered from whatever the first response happened to contain. Where ten things vary the same way, that's one interface with ten implementations decided up front — not ten near-copies discovered later.

I'd rather walk a requirements document and estimate it with the people who wrote it than receive a ticket and guess at what they meant.

I test demand before I build

Before building a billing feature I put up a landing page and took it to real customers, to find out whether anyone wanted it while it was still cheap to be wrong.

And I shelved an internal tool once I found the market already had a good version. Not falling in love with your own idea is harder than it sounds, and cheaper than the alternative.

I prototype in something you can click

My default way of explaining a concept is a working prototype rather than a document, because people judge an interaction properly once they've felt it, and argue about a paragraph describing one.

I stay on the line while I fix it

Through an outage I keep stakeholders updated the whole way rather than going quiet and returning with a resolution. People can plan around a problem they can see.

The same instinct applies to access: when a colleague needed data, I set them up with scoped read-only credentials of their own instead of handing over mine.

Then I write it down

Post-mortems for the failures worth understanding, runbooks for the procedures someone else will have to run at an awkward hour, and a business-logic document a stakeholder can sign off from. Knowledge that only lives in my head is a single point of failure.
