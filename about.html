How I work

An octopus does not smash the jar. It works the lid, feels the mechanism, and gets in. Six habits that show up in everything on this site, and the reason each one earns its keep.

I go to the layer under the tool

When I wanted to understand a managed deploy platform, I rented a small droplet and did it by hand instead, so that a process manager is something I can reason about rather than a service that is either up or down, and so I know what a worker limit actually trades away.

That is why outage work lands. From a dashboard, a full disk is one red number. A level down it is one table holding almost all of it, and then you have something to fix.

The spec comes before the code

Integrations get written against the specification first, not reverse engineered from whatever the first response happened to contain. Where ten things vary the same way, that is one interface with ten implementations decided up front, not ten near copies discovered later.

I would rather walk a requirements document and estimate it with the people who wrote it than receive a ticket and guess at what they meant.

I test demand before I build

Before building a billing feature I put up a landing page and took it to real customers, to find out whether anyone wanted it while it was still cheap to be wrong.

And I shelved an internal tool once I found the market already had a good version. Not falling in love with your own idea is harder than it sounds, and cheaper than the alternative.

I prototype in something you can click

My default way of explaining a concept is a working prototype rather than a document, because people judge an interaction properly once they have felt it, and argue about a paragraph describing one.

I stay on the line while I fix it

Through an outage I keep stakeholders updated the whole way rather than going quiet and returning with a resolution. People can plan around a problem they can see.

The same instinct applies to access. When a colleague needed data, I set them up with scoped read only credentials of their own instead of handing over mine.

Then I write it down

Post mortems for the failures worth understanding, runbooks for the procedures someone else will have to run at an awkward hour, and a business logic document a stakeholder can sign off from. Knowledge that only lives in my head is a single point of failure.
